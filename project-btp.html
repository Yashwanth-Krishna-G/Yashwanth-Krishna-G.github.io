Turbine Blade Cooling Analysis Using Liquid Coolants

CATIA V5ANSYSThermal Engineering B.Tech Project

A conceptual exploration into replacing traditional air-bleed cooling systems with high-performance liquid coolants[cite: 3]. This project focused on the geometric integration of internal cooling passages to push the boundaries of maximum turbine inlet temperatures[cite: 3].

The Conceptual Shift

Traditional air cooling systems impose a hard limitation on maximum turbine inlet temperatures, which directly throttles overall engine efficiency[cite: 3].

To bypass this bottleneck, this foundational study investigated the viability of using high heat-capacity liquid coolants as a direct substitute for air[cite: 3]. By utilizing fluids like Dynalene HC-30, water, and ethylene glycol mixtures[cite: 3], the core idea is that the superior heat transfer properties of liquids could allow gas turbines to operate safely at drastically higher temperatures[cite: 3].

Internal Geometry Integration

Implementing a liquid cooling system requires complex internal routing to maximize surface area contact without compromising the blade's structural integrity[cite: 3].

The turbine blade was designed from scratch in CATIA V5[cite: 3]. The core geometric challenge involved integrating 18 distinct internal coolant passages, each with a diameter of 0.150cm, strategically routed through the titanium blade's cross-section from the leading edge to the trailing edge[cite: 3].

CATIA V5 model illustrating the integration of 18 internal liquid coolant passages[cite: 3].

ANSYS Proof of Concept

The conceptual design was subjected to thermal evaluation in ANSYS[cite: 3]. Using a k-epsilon turbulence model, the blade was exposed to an extreme external environment of 1100K[cite: 3].

Rather than focusing solely on raw temperature drops, the simulation served as a functional proof-of-concept. It validated that strategic liquid routing could successfully extract heat and prevent blade meltdown under high-velocity conditions (170m/s) where traditional air cooling might fail[cite: 3].

ANSYS thermal contour demonstrating heat extraction via internal liquid routing[cite: 3].
